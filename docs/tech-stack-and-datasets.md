# Hybrid stack puts YOLO where it counts

EchoCV should build **Approach A, a hybrid stack**. In the browser, MediaPipe Face Landmarker measures eye contact, smile and head pose. YOLO26n-pose plus a small custom YOLO26n detector measure posture, arms crossed, hand-to-face and reading from notes. On the server, faster-whisper runs a whisper-large-v3-turbo model fine-tuned for fillers, alongside Silero VAD, librosa prosody and a hosted LLM that scores answers against a rubric. YOLO fits the body signals. It has no clean path to gaze, it can only read expression by training on research-only data, and it cannot touch audio at all. So "YOLO where possible" means YOLO for the body and other tools for the face and voice. Every core component is MIT or Apache-licensed except Ultralytics YOLO, which is **AGPL-3.0 including any weights the team trains with it**. That is fine for a public competition repo, but a commercial launch needs an Enterprise licence or a swap to MediaPipe Pose or RTMPose. openSMILE and CrisperWhisper weights are non-commercial and should stay out of the product. With speech recognition self-hosted, the only real running cost is the LLM call, at roughly **US$0.003–0.02 per interview**. Data, not models, is the binding constraint. Stock Whisper drops fillers, and **no public Indonesian filler-labelled audio exists**, so the team must record and label its own mock interviews. Those same recordings can also supply YOLO training frames and HR-rated calibration scores. Of the roughly 150 datasets catalogued below, about twenty are commercially safe and **no interview-rating dataset is among them**. Several gated datasets need requests sent through a lecturer in week 1.

## YOLO measures the body, not the face, gaze or voice

YOLO26 was released on **14 January 2026** as an NMS-free, end-to-end model family that Ultralytics says runs "up to 43% faster" on CPU ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/compare/yolo26-vs-yolov10)). Its smallest pose model, **YOLO26n-pose, has 2.9M parameters, scores 57.2 mAPpose50-95 on COCO, and takes 40.3 ms per frame on CPU with ONNX** ([Ultralytics Pose docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/tasks/pose/)). Since ultralytics 8.4.83, browser deployment goes through `format="litert"`, which produces a `.tflite` file that runs in LiteRT.js on WebGPU and replaces the old TF.js path ([release notes mirror](https://newreleases.io/project/pypi/ultralytics/release/8.4.83); [Ultralytics LiteRT](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/litert)). Google announced LiteRT.js on 9 July 2026, with CPU, WebGPU and experimental WebNN backends ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/litertjs-googles-high-performance-web-ai-inference/)). For body signals, YOLO is therefore a strong fit. Its 17 COCO keypoints, however, stop at the wrists and include **no finger points**, and nothing in the YOLO family reads gaze or sound.

| Signal EchoCV measures | Recommended tool | YOLO's role | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posture / slouching | YOLO26n-pose keypoints; rule comparing shoulder-to-nose distance and head size against a per-user baseline, plus a slouch class in the custom YOLO26n detector | **Core** | Body geometry. No good public frontal "slouch" dataset exists, so the detector class is trained on in-house frames and backed by the calibrated rule |
| Arms crossed | Pose rule (each wrist past the midline toward the opposite elbow), or a class in the custom YOLO26n detector | **Core** | A single-frame visual pattern that a detector handles well |
| Touching face | A class in the custom YOLO26n detector, backed by wrist-to-nose/ear distance normalised by shoulder width plus the face box | **Core** | COCO-17 has no fingers, so wrist distance is an approximation |
| Fidgeting | Motion of pose keypoints over time (a rule or a small temporal classifier) | Supplies keypoints only | Fidgeting is movement over time, which a per-frame detector cannot see |
| Looking down at notes / holding notes | Custom YOLO26n detector | **Core** | A single-frame object or pose pattern |
| Head pose and nodding | MediaPipe facial transformation matrix | None | Already produced by Face Landmarker |
| Eye contact | MediaPipe iris landmarks plus head yaw/pitch, with a 3–5 s per-user calibration | None | No YOLO gaze model exists, and every major gaze dataset is non-commercial |
| Smile / expression | MediaPipe's 52 blendshapes | None | A YOLO expression head would need RAF-DB-style data, which is research-only and Western-skewed |
| Face detection | BlazeFace inside Face Landmarker | None (avoid) | YOLO face weights are GPL-3.0 and trained on WIDER FACE (CC BY-NC-ND) |
| Fillers, speaking rate, pauses | faster-whisper + fine-tuned turbo; Silero VAD | None | Audio |
| Pitch and volume | librosa `pyin` and RMS | None | Audio |
| Answer content (STAR, relevance) | LLM rubric | None | Language |

The pattern is simple. YOLO handles the signal wherever it is body geometry or a visible object in one frame. MediaPipe handles everything that lives in the face. Speech and language models handle the rest. MediaPipe Face Landmarker returns **478 3D landmarks including the iris, 52 blendshape scores and a facial transformation matrix** in a single in-browser call ([Google AI Edge](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker)). Eye contact should therefore be a calibrated rule rather than a trained model. A frame counts as eye contact when the head's yaw and pitch from the transformation matrix and the iris offset inside the eye socket both fall within thresholds. Those thresholds are set during a 3–5 second "look at the camera" step, because laptop users naturally look at the screen below the webcam. EchoCV then reports the percentage of speaking time spent in eye contact. The blendshape names (`eyeLook*`, `mouthSmileLeft/Right`) and iris indices (468–477) come from general knowledge of the MediaPipe topology, so **verify them against the Blendshape V2 model card** before coding them in. If a learned gaze model is wanted later, the MIT-licensed **L2CS-Gaze360-LiteRT** runs in-browser through LiteRT.js. It is a 47.9 MB fp16 download that needs a face crop ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/L2CS-Gaze360-LiteRT)).

Four practical details matter in week 1. First, **LiteRT export runs only on Linux x86_64 and macOS**, so a team on Windows should export in Colab, Kaggle or WSL2 ([Ultralytics LiteRT](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/litert)). Second, the dynamic-int8 `w8a32` export needs no calibration data. Third, ONNX Runtime Web with the WebGPU execution provider remains a fallback ([ONNX Runtime docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/tutorials/web/ep-webgpu.html)). Fourth, the export docs leave unclear whether YOLO26 pose exports default to end-to-end output, so test the `nms` settings early ([Ultralytics Export](https://docs.ultralytics.com/modes/export/)). The speed claims that LiteRT.js is "~2× faster than ONNX Runtime Web" and "up to 3x" faster than other runtimes come from the vendors, not independent benchmarks. No benchmark exists for Face Landmarker and YOLO26n-pose running together on an RTX 3050 Ti laptop. Posture does not need 30 FPS, so sampling pose at 5–10 FPS leaves headroom for the face model.

## Hybrid Approach A beats YOLO-for-everything and API-only

The team discussed three architectures. Approach A, the hybrid, wins on licensing, cost, accuracy for Indonesian faces, and the one feature no hosted service delivers well: filler detection.

| Dimension | **A: Hybrid (recommended)** | B: YOLO-for-everything | C: API-only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vision | MediaPipe face/iris/blendshapes + YOLO26n-pose + custom YOLO26n detector, all in the browser | YOLO face detector, YOLO expression classifier, YOLO-based eye-contact classifier, YOLO pose and behaviours | Kept minimal in the browser (the cost model assumes vision stays client-side) |
| Speech | Self-hosted faster-whisper + turbo fine-tuned for fillers, Silero VAD, librosa | Same as A, because YOLO cannot process audio | Hosted ASR (GPT-Transcribe / GPT-Live-Transcribe) or audio sent straight to Gemini |
| Answer scoring | Hosted LLM rubric (Gemini Flash-Lite or GPT-5.6 Luna) | Same as A | Same hosted LLM |
| Training required | Custom YOLO26n detector + Whisper filler fine-tune | A's training plus a face detector, an expression classifier and a gaze classifier | None |
| Cost per 15-min session | ≈ $0.003–0.02 (self-hosted ASR); ≈ $0.05–0.07 with OpenAI batch ASR | Similar to A, plus more GPU time for training | ≈ $0.17–0.20 with streaming ASR |
| Fillers ("eee", "hmm", "um") | Fine-tuned verbatim model | Same as A | Hosted ASR does not reliably emit filler tokens |
| Licensing exposure | AGPL on YOLO only | AGPL + GPL face weights + non-commercial training data for face, expression and gaze | Vendor API terms; student data leaves the device |
| Main risk | Indonesian filler data must be collected | Weeks of extra labelling on biased, research-only data | Cost scales per session, and the core feature is weak |

Approach B fails on three separate counts. The ready-made YOLO face models are GPL-3.0: akanametov/yolo-face already ships YOLO26-n/s face weights trained on WIDER FACE ([GitHub](https://github.com/akanametov/yolo-face)), and WIDER FACE itself is **CC BY-NC-ND 4.0** ([WIDER FACE](http://shuoyang1213.me/WIDERFACE/)). An expression head needs a facial-expression dataset, and the standard one, RAF-DB, is non-commercial and requires a university email ([RAF-DB](http://www.whdeng.cn/RAF/model1.html)). It is also demographically skewed: its test set is **77.4% Caucasian and 15.5% Asian**, and the Asian subgroup reached only **60.0% class-wise accuracy** without augmentation ([Xu et al., arXiv 2007.10075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.10075)). A single-head "detect face and classify expression" YOLO design does exist in FER-YOLO-Mamba, which was evaluated on RAF-DB and SFEW, but its repo carries no licence ([arXiv 2405.01828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01828)). Every major gaze dataset is non-commercial (see the catalogue), and the only YOLO-format hand-keypoint set is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([Ultralytics](https://docs.ultralytics.com/datasets/pose/hand-keypoints/)). B therefore adds three or four training jobs, all on research-only data, to replicate what MediaPipe already gives for free with zero training. It still needs the full speech pipeline anyway.

Approach C is the fastest to build and the weakest product. OpenAI's streaming **GPT-Live-Transcribe costs $0.017 per minute** and the batch **GPT-Transcribe $0.0045 per minute** ([OpenAI pricing](https://openai.com/api/pricing/)). Ten minutes of candidate speech therefore costs about $0.17 or $0.045 before any scoring. Sending audio straight into Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite at $0.50 per million audio tokens works out to about $0.0075 per ten minutes ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)), but only if the 25 tokens-per-second rate quoted for the Live model also applies to Flash-Lite, which was not verified. The decisive weakness is fillers. Hosted ASR targets clean transcripts and does not reliably emit filler tokens. Nyra's own benchmark puts ElevenLabs Scribe v2 at **79.2 disfluency F1, against 87.8–93.5 for verbatim-trained CrisperWhisper 2.0** ([CrisperWhisper GitHub](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)), and whether Indonesian is among the benchmarked languages is unconfirmed. C also leaves the team with no model of its own to show judges, and it ignores the YOLO preference. Approach A keeps C's one strong idea, a hosted LLM for rubric scoring, and self-hosts everything else.

Fine-tuning will not fit on 4 GB and belongs on a T4. Hosted LLM scoring is cheap enough to ignore. With about 5,000 input tokens (transcript, rubric and metrics JSON) and 1,500 output tokens, a session costs **about $0.0035 on Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite and about $0.003 on GPT-5.6 Luna** ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [OpenAI pricing](https://openai.com/api/pricing/)). None of the open-weight judges fits the 4 GB GPU, since a 9B model at 4-bit needs roughly 5–6 GB. Their value is an "Indonesian AI" story for the competition and an offline fallback, with SEA-HELM's leaderboard as the way to choose between them ([SEA-HELM](https://github.com/aisingapore/SEA-HELM)). The model card itself states a **Llama 3.1 70B Instruct base under the Llama 3.1 Community License** ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/GoToCompany/Llama-Sahabat-AI-v2-70B-IT)), so follow the card.

### Three licences decide whether EchoCV can ever be sold

**Ultralytics AGPL-3.0** is the largest licensing obligation in the stack. Ultralytics states that "All Ultralytics YOLO trained models fall under the AGPL-3.0 License by default". Compliance means "publicly releasing the complete corresponding source code for the entire derivative work, including the larger application", and that applies even when the software is deployed "through a SaaS platform, API, or other private system". An Enterprise License is required for "Any commercial product or service". Pricing is "tailored to each organization's size", and no student, academic or startup programme is advertised ([Ultralytics License](https://ultralytics.com/license)). For the competition, the fix is to publish the repo under AGPL-3.0. For any commercial path, either buy the Enterprise licence or swap pose to MediaPipe Pose Landmarker and the behaviour detector to a non-Ultralytics model. The licences of alternative detectors (RT-DETR, RTMDet, YOLOX) were not verified.

**openSMILE** is free for "private, research, and educational use", but "It is not allowed to use the open-source version of openSMILE for any sort of commercial product". Commercial licences go through audEERING ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/opensmile)). librosa covers EchoCV's prosody needs (pitch via `pyin`, volume via RMS) without that restriction, so openSMILE should not enter the codebase at all.

**CrisperWhisper** proves the filler-transcription method works, but its weights cannot ship. The v1 model card lists **cc-by-nc-4.0** and guarantees performance only in English and German ([HF](https://huggingface.co/nyralabs/CrisperWhisper)). The 2.0 release keeps the inference code under MIT but puts the **model weights under a Non-Commercial Research License**, with commercial use requiring an agreement with Nyra. It claims support for "most languages Whisper supports" across 10 benchmarked languages, and whether Indonesian is among them was not confirmed ([GitHub](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)). Use it as an English benchmark in the competition write-up and copy its recipe, but not its weights.

## Whisper drops fillers and no Indonesian filler audio exists

Filler counting is the feature users will notice most, and it is the one off-the-shelf tools handle worst. Whisper is trained to output the clean, "intended" transcript, so "eee", "hmm" and "um" tend to vanish. That is why CrisperWhisper had to retrain Whisper large-v3 on AMI, PodcastFillers and Common Voice, **re-purposing tokens for "uh" and "um"** so that filled pauses are transcribed canonically ([arXiv 2408.16589](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.16589v1)). The fix is known: fine-tune on verbatim transcripts in which fillers appear as explicit tokens. The problem is that the needed training data does not exist in Indonesian. Across speech, disfluency and stuttering corpora, **no Indonesian or multilingual filled-pause dataset was found**. The only Indonesian corpus documented to contain fillers is Tazakka et al.'s natural Indonesian-English code-switching set, whose speech "include[s] verbal fillers", and it has not been released ([SIGUL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigul-1.18.pdf)). The larger IDSV evaluation set (80.54 h, including talk shows and podcasts) is also unreleased ([arXiv 2410.08828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08828)). The one free conversational corpus, MagicHub IndoCSC, is just 4.54 h from four speakers under CC BY-NC-ND, and its pages do not say whether fillers are tagged ([MagicHub](https://magichub.com/datasets/indonesian-conversational-speech-corpus/)).

The team must therefore **record and verbatim-label its own mock interviews**. The IDSV study supports this: it found speaking style to be the single most influential factor in Indonesian ASR error, and fine-tuned Whisper gave the best WER and CER ([arXiv 2410.08828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08828)). The protocol borrows CrisperWhisper's trick. Map vocalised pauses to canonical tokens such as `[EEE]`, `[HMM]` and `[UM]`. Transcribe Indonesian, English and code-switched answers exactly as spoken. Mix in English verbatim data from AMI (CC BY 4.0, commercial use allowed) and DisfluencySpeech (Apache-2.0, with `{F uh}` markup). Then fine-tune. The research suggests that **3–10 hours** of in-house verbatim audio would be worth more than any public set for fine-tuning, and even more for evaluation. That figure is a judgement, not a measured threshold. Lexical fillers such as "apa", "kayak", "gitu", "anu", "jadi" and "seperti" are real words that Whisper transcribes anyway, so they should be counted with transcript rules or the LLM rather than retrained into the acoustic model.

The fine-tune is feasible on free GPUs. The Hugging Face Audio Course fine-tunes whisper-small on a **16 GB Colab T4** (lr 1e-5, batch 16, fp16, 4,000 steps in 3–5 hours) ([HF Audio Course](https://huggingface.co/learn/audio-course/en/chapter5/fine-tuning)). LoRA with INT8 trains Whisper-large-class models in **under 8 GB of VRAM**, shrinks the checkpoint from 6 GB to 60 MB, and reached 14.01 WER against 13.64 for a full fine-tune ([PEFT 0.2.0 notes](https://newreleases.io/project/pypi/peft/release/0.2.0)). Merge the LoRA weights and convert them with `ct2-transformers-converter` for faster-whisper ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/faster-whisper/)). The same pass yields every other speaking metric: speaking rate as words per second of speech, pauses as Silero VAD gaps longer than about 0.7 s or 2 s, volume as RMS in dB, and pitch variability as the standard deviation of `pyin` f0 over voiced frames. cahya/whisper-medium-id, at **3.83% WER on Common Voice 11 (against 12.62% for the base model)** ([HF](https://huggingface.co/cahya/whisper-medium-id)), is the Indonesian baseline to beat. The headline metric for the filler model is filler F1 on a held-out in-house set, not WER on read speech.

The same data gap exists on the vision and scoring sides. No public frontal-webcam dataset labels touching face, reading notes, fidgeting or slouching in interview framing. The Indonesian face datasets are tiny: IMED has 15 subjects ([IMED](https://imed.cs.ui.ac.id)), and DIKE-Face's size is unstated ([UGM](https://computervision.mipa.ugm.ac.id/?p=1695)). Every human-rated interview dataset is English. So one consented recording protocol should feed all three in-house datasets at once. Webcam frames from the recordings go to Roboflow for the YOLO26n detector; a few hundred to about 1,500 labelled frames across 4–5 classes is a reasonable first iteration. Audio goes to verbatim filler transcription. A few dozen complete interviews are rated by 2–3 HR practitioners on 1–5 or 1–7 Likert scales, following the MIT and AVI designs, to calibrate EchoCV's overall score ([arXiv 1504.03425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.03425)). Recruit participants with and without hijab and glasses and under varied lighting, since those are the local variations DIKE-Face was built to capture.

## Eight weeks: ship rules first, train on in-house data second

The plan front-loads everything that needs no training and starts data collection on day one. Training in week 3 then already has two weeks of recordings to work with. The week boundaries are the researcher's judgement, not sourced estimates.

There are three main risks. The first is the Indonesian filler data, which no public source can replace. The second is browser GPU load on low-end laptops running two vision models; mitigate it by sampling frames. The third is the AGPL obligation, which requires keeping the repo public. Framing scores as practice feedback is also required by the terms of several research datasets, and ChaLearn's own authors warn that such systems "are not ready to be used… for anything that determines opportunities for people, such as job interviews" ([arXiv 2011.14906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.14906)).

## Dataset catalogue: about 150 public datasets grouped by purpose

Each dataset name carries a verification marker. **V** means the page was fetched on 5 October 2026 and the row's facts come from it. **P** means the page was fetched but some facts, marked "(U)", come from a mirror or general knowledge. **S** means the link only surfaced in search results or an API listing and was not opened, so the details are unverified. Hugging Face links on S rows were built from repo IDs returned by the HF API. **U** means unverified: background knowledge, a failed fetch, or no official page found. In the commercial-safe column, "Yes" means the stated licence permits commercial use, usually with attribution. "No" covers non-commercial, research-only, paid or unreleased data. "Unclear → No" means no licence was found, so treat the data as research-only until the authors confirm otherwise.

## Conclusion

The YOLO preference and the product's needs are compatible once YOLO is scoped to the body. The deeper finding is that EchoCV's hard problem is data, not architecture. Every component in Approach A is downloadable, permissively licensed apart from Ultralytics, and runs on free hardware. The three things that make EchoCV specific to Indonesian IT students are Indonesian filler detection, behaviour detection on Southeast Asian faces in laptop-webcam framing, and score calibration against Indonesian HR judgement. No public dataset supplies any of the three. A single consented recording protocol (bilingual mock interviews, captured on laptop webcams, transcribed verbatim with filler tokens, and rated by a few HR practitioners) closes all three gaps at once. It also produces something no public catalogue contains: the first rated Indonesian spoken-interview corpus, which is a stronger competition contribution than any model choice.

A second, less obvious implication concerns licensing. It flows through trained weights, not just code. Ultralytics makes YOLO-trained weights AGPL. L2CS, cahya/whisper-medium-id and RTMPose carry permissive model licences but were trained partly on research-only data. Any EchoCV model fine-tuned on PodcastFillers labels, MagicHub or INDspeech inherits the same ambiguity. The cheapest insurance is bookkeeping: tag every checkpoint with the datasets it touched, from week 1. If EchoCV outlives the competition, the commercial version can then be retrained on the safe shortlist plus in-house data, with no forensic work to reconstruct what went into each model.
